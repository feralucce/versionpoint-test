Trace a leak

Find out which copy a leaked release came from, and who you gave it to, from the hidden mark every copy carries.

Why it matters

Sooner or later a release, or one paragraph of it, turns up before it should: on a community blog, in a reporter's inbox, in a partner's newsletter. Before anyone points a finger, you need to know which copy it came from and who you gave that copy to. Guessing wrong costs you a working relationship; Trace a leak gives you a fact to start from.

Watch

Every copy carries its own mark

Every review copy and every clean copy VersionPoint makes carries its own hidden mark: a short run of invisible characters at the end of each paragraph that has real text in it. Each copy's mark is different. The mark doesn't change how the release looks, Word's word count or what Find finds, so the person you send it to won't notice it.

VersionPoint knows who each review copy went to, because you named the reviewer when you sent it. For a clean copy, it only knows if you tell it.

The "Trace a leak" page

What the result means

What you seeWhat it means
A copy is named, like Clean copy for Riverton Transit Authority, made by Sam Sample or Round 1, sent to Riley Planner (riley@example.gov)The text came from that copy. It tells you whose hands the copy started in, not who posted it: it may have been forwarded since.
A VersionPoint mark was found … but it isn't one of the copies on this computer.The text came from a VersionPoint copy, but not one made here. It may come from someone else's VersionPoint: another office that uses it.
No VersionPoint mark was found.The trace can't tell. The text may have been retyped, printed and scanned, or cleaned of invisible characters, or it may not come from a VersionPoint copy.
What the marks survive, and what they don't

The marks go wherever the characters go. They survive copy and paste, forwarding, and most web pages: a paragraph pasted into an email, a blog post or a new Word file still carries its mark.

They don't survive retyping, printing and scanning, screenshots, or tools that clean invisible characters out of text. Some web publishing tools do that too.

The mark sits at the end of each paragraph, so a quote that stops partway through a paragraph doesn't carry it. Paste the whole paragraph, or the whole leaked piece, as you found it.

Try it

Start on Documents, where lesson 8 left you. You'll need Word for step 3, and in step 5 the review copy you downloaded in lesson 2, Lake Shuttle news release - for Riley Planner - round 1.docx.

  1. Open Lake Shuttle news release (version 3) and choose Clean copy. In Who is this copy for?, type Riverton Transit Authority.
  2. Choose Download a clean copy. The form adds Downloaded: Lake Shuttle news release.docx (for Riverton Transit Authority). Choose Close. (If you kept lesson 7's clean copy in the same folder, your browser may add a number to the new file's name.)
  3. Open the clean copy you just downloaded in Word. Triple-click inside the paragraph that starts The shuttle uses four buses to select all of it, and copy it.
  4. Back in VersionPoint, choose Trace a leak at the top. Paste into Paste the leaked text and choose Trace. Under What the pasted text carries it says Clean copy for Riverton Transit Authority, made by Sam Sample, with Lake Shuttle news release, version 3.
  5. Drop your lesson 2 review copy on Or drop the leaked file (or click it and choose the file). It says Round 1, sent to Riley Planner (riley@example.gov), from version 1.
  6. Clear the Paste the leaked text box, type a sentence of your own about the shuttle, and choose Trace. It says No VersionPoint mark was found.

Check your work

Watch out for

A trace is a lead, not proof on its own. It names the copy and who you gave it to, not who posted it: a partner may have forwarded it inside their own office. Check with them, and with what else you know, before anyone is accused.
Retyped text carries no mark. Nor do screenshots, print-and-scan, or text run through a tool that cleans invisible characters. No VersionPoint mark was found. doesn't clear anyone; it means the trace can't tell.
Several copies named means the text was put together from several copies. The page says More than one copy's mark was found: the text was put together from several copies. Look at which paragraph came from which copy before you draw a conclusion.
Leave Who is this copy for? blank and the trace can't name anyone. It will only say Clean copy, made by Sam Sample. Fill it in for every clean copy that leaves the office, one copy per recipient.
